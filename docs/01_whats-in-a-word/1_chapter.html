1  Inequality: What’s in a Word?

The word “inequality” is used loosely: sometimes it means poverty, sometimes unfairness, sometimes the gap between the rich and everyone else. But inequality always implies a comparison: between individuals, groups, regions, or countries, and with respect to something. This chapter sorts out the concepts, shows how inequality is measured, and asks how we can analyse where it comes from. It ends with the question that frames the whole module: which differences do we consider justified, and why?

1.1 Why Should We Care About Inequality?

NoteOpening question

Before reading on, finish this sentence with one word: “Inequality is …” Write down two or three answers. You will come back to them at the end of the chapter.

A sense of fairness

Start with a short clip from a famous experiment by primatologist Frans de Waal. Two capuchin monkeys exchange a stone for food. Both are content as long as both receive cucumber. Then one of them gets a grape for the same effort.

What had been acceptable a moment ago becomes unacceptable once a neighbour gets a better reward for the same work. A sense of fairness seems to run deep. (A note on the experiment: the monkeys live together in a social group and were only briefly separated into neighbouring cages for the test. Animal research nonetheless raises ethical questions, and noticing them is part of thinking critically about how knowledge is produced.)

A question of legitimacy

Inequality is not only a matter of feelings. Thomas Piketty opens Capital and Ideology with a claim about legitimacy:

“Every human society must justify its inequalities: unless reasons for them are found, the whole political and social structure stands in danger of collapse.” (Piketty 2020)

The idea is old. Plato argued that a state which wants to avoid civil disintegration must not allow extreme poverty or extreme wealth, and that the legislator should set acceptable limits to both. In The Laws, he proposes a fixed upper limit to how much property any citizen may own relative to the poorest. When disparities grow too large, they erode trust, weaken cooperation, and fuel social fragmentation.

A question for sustainability

Inequality is also an ecological question. How income and wealth are distributed shapes how societies consume, pollute, and use resources. Chancel (2022) shows that the global top 10% of emitters are responsible for almost half of all greenhouse gas emissions, while the bottom half of the world population causes about a tenth. The World Inequality Report 2026 adds a second perspective: if emissions are attributed to the owners of the companies and assets that cause them, the wealthiest 10% account for 77% of the emissions linked to private capital ownership, and the poorest half for only 3% (World Inequality Lab 2026).

This is also why reducing inequality within and among countries is one of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 10). Of all the goals, SDG 10 is perhaps the most political, because it touches on the question of who gets what, and who decides.

ImportantInequality and sustainability transformations

Inequality is both a cause and a consequence of the polycrisis: it drives overconsumption at the top, limits the capabilities of those at the bottom, and undermines the trust and cooperation that any transformation needs.

1.2 Equality of What?

In his Tanner Lecture of 1979, Amartya Sen asked a deceptively simple question: Equality of what? Before we can talk about fairness, we have to decide two things.

NoteWhere and among whom?
  • Within countries: between individuals, genders, ethnic groups, generations, or regions
  • Between countries: comparing average incomes of nations
  • Global inequality: among all people of the world, regardless of where they live
NoteOf what?

Income, wealth, health, education, opportunities, quality of life, voice and participation, exposure to climate risks, and more.

The choice of the focal variable (what we compare) and the reference unit (among whom) already shapes the answer. Income inequality in Switzerland, wealth inequality in Switzerland, and global income inequality tell three different stories.

Outcome or opportunity?

Much of the debate boils down to two perspectives:

  • Inequality of outcome occurs when individuals do not have the same level of material wealth or living conditions. It is usually measured in monetary terms.
  • Inequality of opportunity asks whether people have the same chances, regardless of circumstances they did not choose, such as their parents’ income, their gender, or where they were born.

Advocates of equality of opportunity often argue that as long as everyone has the same chances, nobody can complain about the results. But today’s outcomes are tomorrow’s opportunities: the income of your parents shapes your education, your health, and your network. A report by UNDP calls the opposition between the two a “false dichotomy”: outcome and opportunity are two sides of the same coin, and policies against inequality must address both (UNDP 2013).

The capability approach: from equality to equity

Sen’s capability approach shifts the focus from what people have to what they are able to do and to be.

  • Functionings are the beings and doings people value: being healthy, being educated, taking part in the life of the community.
  • Capabilities are the real freedoms to achieve these functionings.
  • Conversion factors (personal, social, and environmental) determine whether resources such as income or public services can be turned into functionings.

“A disabled person will likely find it harder to achieve important and worthwhile goals than will a nondisabled person, even assuming they have access to equivalent bundles of resources.” (Sen, cited in Robeyns 2005)

Two people with the same income may have very different real freedoms if one of them faces discrimination, has a disability, or lacks access to transport. Equalising income is therefore not enough. The capability approach moves us from equality (everyone receives the same input) to equity (what does each person need to reach a comparable level of well-being?). Martha Nussbaum extended the approach with a list of ten central capabilities that every society should secure for all its members, from bodily health to control over one’s environment.

1.3 Dimensions and Measures

Across dimensions and spaces

Two further distinctions help to sort out what a given number tells us.

Distinction Meaning
Vertical inequality Inequality among individuals or households, for example between the richest and poorest 10%
Horizontal inequality Inequality among groups defined by identity, such as ethnicity, religion, gender, region, or age (Stewart 2005)
Absolute inequality The size of the gap between groups, measured in money (for example, dollars)
Relative inequality The proportional difference between groups, measured as ratios or shares

Vertical inequality tells us about the overall distribution. Horizontal inequality makes structural exclusion visible: it matters for social cohesion and conflict whether poverty is spread across society or concentrated in one ethnic group or region (Stewart 2005).

The absolute versus relative distinction will become central in chapter 2. A simple example shows why: a 10% increase on an income of 1,000 dollars is 100 dollars. A 10% increase on an income of 1,000,000 dollars is 100,000 dollars. In relative terms, both grew equally. In absolute terms, the gap widened by 99,900 dollars.

The Gini coefficient

The most widely used measure of inequality is the Gini coefficient. It is based on the Lorenz curve, which plots the cumulative share of the population (from poorest to richest) against their cumulative share of income. If everyone had the same income, the curve would follow the diagonal line of perfect equality.

The Gini coefficient is the area between the line of perfect equality and the Lorenz curve (area A), divided by the whole area under the diagonal (A + B). It ranges from 0 (everyone has the same) to 1 (one person has everything).

The Gini is useful, but it has well-known limits (Hickel 2016; Trapeznikova 2019):

  • It is a relative measure. If all incomes grow by 10%, the Gini stays the same, even though the absolute gap between rich and poor grows.
  • It depends on what is measured. Values differ for wages, market income, disposable income (after taxes and transfers), consumption, and wealth. Wealth Ginis are typically much higher than income Ginis.
  • It is over-sensitive to the middle and captures changes at the very top and the very bottom poorly, because it is a calculation of an area.

The Palma ratio

The Palma ratio compares the income share of the richest 10% with that of the poorest 40%. It builds on the observation that the share of the middle classes (deciles 5 to 9) tends to be fairly stable across countries, so that most differences between countries come from the tails. In Figure 1.1, the richest 10% in society B earn 4.3 times as much as the poorest 40% together. The Palma is easy to communicate and responds to what happens at the top and bottom.

TipGood practice

No single number captures inequality. Complement the Gini with an absolute measure or the Palma ratio, and say clearly whether you measure income or wealth, before or after taxes, and within or between countries.

1.4 Individual or Structural?

How should we explain inequality? Two broad approaches compete.

  • Individual approach: inequality is analysed at the level of the individual. Economic inequalities are primarily the result of personal attributes and efforts: education, talent, choices, hard work. This is the dominant view in neoclassical (marginalist) economics.
  • Structural approach: inequalities are built into the structure of society and into social positions, for example through citizenship (where you are born) or your position in relation to the means of production (whether you own capital or sell your labour). This view is rooted in classical political economy and in post-Keynesian and Marxian traditions.

Think of a running race in which one runner starts on a clear track while three others have hurdles in their lanes. No matter how hard the three try, they tend to fall behind. The individual approach looks at the runners; the structural approach looks at the track.

From effort to structure

How much of your income is your own doing? Branko Milanovic, one of the leading researchers on global inequality, gives a striking answer:

“When you ask how much of the total income […] can be explained by the variable of the country of birth, you basically get an answer of 0.6. […] 60% of your lifetime income is determined by your place of birth.” (Milanovic, interview 2019)

Adding the income position of your parents, Milanovic estimates that about 80% of income differences across the world can be explained by two factors nobody chooses: country of birth (about 60%) and parents’ income (about 20%). The remaining 20% is left for effort, luck, and everything else. Milanovic calls the premium of being born in a rich country a “citizenship rent.” These numbers challenge the idea that inequality mainly reflects merit.

Position in relation to the means of production

Two hundred years ago, David Ricardo put distribution at the very centre of economics:

“The produce of the earth, all that is derived from its surface by the united application of labour, machinery, and capital, is divided among three classes of the community; namely, the proprietor of the land, the owner of the stock or capital necessary for its cultivation, and the labourers by whose industry it is cultivated. […] To determine the laws which regulate this distribution, is the principal problem in Political Economy.” (Ricardo 1817, Preface)

Landowners receive rent, capital owners profit, and workers wages. Who owns productive assets therefore shapes everything from income patterns to political power.

From the late 19th century, the marginalist revolution shifted the focus of economics from class-based distribution to individual utility and marginal productivity. In this view, each factor of production is paid according to what it adds to output: a worker who adds two cars a day is paid the value of those two cars. Distribution becomes a technical outcome of markets rather than a question of power.

Michał Kalecki (1954) brought distribution back into macroeconomics. In his model, “workers spend what they get and capitalists get what they spend.” Because workers spend most of their income, a higher wage share tends to raise demand. A higher profit share can boost investment, but only if profits are reinvested; otherwise it leads to weak demand and stagnation. Distribution is therefore not only a moral question but a question of macroeconomic stability.

1.5 Beliefs and Theories of Justice

Different concepts and perspectives identify different causes of inequality, and therefore lead to different solutions. Which concepts we choose depends on our beliefs, our worldviews, our social backgrounds, and the theories of justice we hold. Even the measurement of inequality is not value-free. As Anthony Atkinson (1973) put it, conventional measures “embody quite arbitrary values about the distribution of income”; that no explicit judgement is needed to compute them “simply obscures” this fact.

Three families of theories of justice

Family Core idea View of inequality Thinkers
Libertarian Heavy weight on individual freedom and private property; the market is the best allocation mechanism Not a problem as long as it results from free choices and voluntary exchange Milton and Rose Friedman (Free to Choose, 1980), Robert Nozick (Anarchy, State, and Utopia, 1974)
Liberal egalitarian Society is analysed in terms of its individual members; markets are efficient but produce poverty and inequality that the state can and should reduce Acceptable only under fair conditions John Rawls (A Theory of Justice, 1971), Ronald Dworkin
Collectivist Society consists of social classes defined by their relation to the means of production Structural, produced by the economic system itself Marxian traditions, John Roemer; communitarian critiques such as Michael Sandel

Rawls invites us to imagine a veil of ignorance: if you did not know where in society you would be born, which rules would you choose? His difference principle allows inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged. Sen criticised Rawls for focusing on resources (“primary goods”) and overlooking the diversity of people’s ability to convert them into a good life, which brings us back to the capability approach.

Utilitarianism (Jeremy Bentham, John Stuart Mill) offers yet another lens: the just action is the one that produces the greatest good for the greatest number. It has little to say about how that good is distributed.

What do we believe?

Here is a thought experiment used in many classrooms. Divide the population of your country into five groups of equal size, from the poorest 20% to the richest 20%. Before looking at any data:

  1. How do you think wealth is distributed among the five groups?
  2. How should it ideally be distributed?

Then compare your answers with the data. For Switzerland, the richest 20% own the overwhelming majority of net wealth, while the poorest 20% own close to nothing or have net debts (SGB 2018). Studies consistently find that people underestimate how unequal their societies are, and that their ideal distribution is far more equal than both their estimate and reality. We return to this in section 4.5.

Conclusion

Accounting for inequality implies a debate about the level of difference that is ethically justifiable, politically negotiable, and economically viable. (Bader and Bieri 2017)

ImportantKey takeaways of chapter 1
  • Inequality always means a comparison: of something (income, wealth, capabilities) among someone (individuals, groups, countries).
  • Outcome and opportunity are two sides of the same coin.
  • Every measure makes value judgements. The Gini is relative and insensitive to the extremes; complement it.
  • Individual explanations focus on effort; structural explanations show that birthplace and parents’ income explain most income differences worldwide.
  • Our theories of justice shape what we see as a problem and which solutions we consider.
