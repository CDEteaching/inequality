2  A Tale of Two Stories

Now that we have sorted out what inequality means, we look at how it has evolved. This chapter focuses on income inequality and on the relationship between economic growth, poverty, and inequality. You will meet two stories about recent decades. Both are based on the same data. Your task is to work out what each story highlights, what it hides, and which one you find more convincing.

2.1 Growth, Poverty, and Inequality

We live by stories

In Sapiens, the historian Yuval Noah Harari argues that what distinguishes humans from other animals is our ability to believe in shared stories:

“You could never convince a monkey to give you a banana by promising him limitless bananas after death in monkey heaven.” (Harari 2014)

Because we believe in stories (nations, laws, money, markets), we can cooperate with countless strangers. The same holds for inequality. People accept hierarchies because they believe in collective narratives: meritocracy, national progress, market freedom. Which story about inequality do you believe in?

Is growth the solution or the problem?

Development strategies rest on assumptions about how three things are linked: economic growth, poverty, and inequality.

  • In one story, growth is the solution. A rising tide lifts all boats: growth reduces poverty, and inequality either falls over time or does not matter much as long as the poor are better off. This story underlies the trickle-down argument and is closest to the libertarian view of justice.
  • In the other story, growth as it is currently organised is part of the problem. Its gains are captured by those who already own assets, inequality rises, and poverty reduction stays far below what growth could deliver. This story is closer to liberal egalitarian and collectivist views.

The famous Kuznets curve (1955) proposed a middle ground: inequality first rises with industrialisation and then falls as economies mature. Later research has largely failed to confirm an automatic turning point. Where inequality fell, as in Europe and North America after 1945, this was driven by wars, crises, and deliberate policies such as progressive taxation and strong trade unions, not by growth as such (Piketty 2014; Scheidel 2017).

Since the 1980s, two stories coexist. One is of progress: hundreds of millions of people have left extreme poverty, largely thanks to growth in China, India, and Vietnam. The other is of polarisation: income and wealth have become concentrated at the very top, and many societies are drifting apart. The next two sections examine both.

2.2 Story One: The Elephant Curve

One of the most famous graphs in inequality research shows how the incomes of people across the global income distribution grew between 1988 (just before the fall of the Berlin Wall) and 2008 (the global financial crisis). The horizontal axis ranks all people in the world from the poorest (left) to the richest (right). The vertical axis shows the growth of their real income, adjusted for purchasing power, over these 20 years.

Because of its shape, the graph became known as the elephant curve.

NoteBefore reading on

Look at the curve for one minute. Who gained most from globalisation between 1988 and 2008? Who gained least?

Two points stand out:

  • Point A, around the middle of the distribution, marks the largest relative gains, of 70 to 80%. These are mostly people in the emerging middle classes of Asia, above all in China.
  • Point B, around the 80th percentile, marks almost no gains. These are mostly lower middle classes in Western Europe and North America.

The very top of the distribution also gained strongly, and the very poorest gained relatively little.

The story this graph seems to tell is a story of progress and convergence: many of the poorer people of the world (on the left) gained more, in percentage terms, than most of the rich (on the right). Global inequality between countries has fallen, and extreme poverty has declined.

It also helps explain political tensions in rich countries: while hundreds of millions in Asia moved up, many in the lower middle classes of the Global North felt left behind. Populist backlashes and disillusionment with globalisation partly feed on this experience.

2.3 Story Two: The Absolute View

Now look at the exact same data from a different angle. The red line shows not the percentage growth but the growth of income in dollars for each percentile.

The picture changes dramatically. In absolute terms, the lowest 90% of the world population saw only small income gains, while the richest gained far more than anyone else. A large percentage increase on a very small income is still a small amount of money. Relative convergence can coexist with absolute divergence.

If global cooperation and growth produce gains, the question of how these gains are shared is unavoidable. From the absolute perspective, the story is not one of convergence, but of a world drifting apart.

Which poverty line?

The same tension appears in debates on poverty. In 2019, the anthropologist Jason Hickel published an article with the title “Bill Gates says poverty is decreasing. He couldn’t be more wrong.” The argument raises three questions you can ask of any poverty statistic:

  • The China effect. A large part of the global decline in extreme poverty happened in one country, China, under a development model far from the free-market recipe usually credited with it.
  • Which poverty line? The World Bank’s international extreme poverty line (USD 1.90 a day until 2022, USD 2.15 until 2025, now USD 3.00 in 2021 prices) is very low. The World Bank also publishes higher lines for lower and upper middle-income countries (USD 4.20 and USD 8.30 a day), and some researchers argue for lines of USD 10 or more to capture a decent life. With higher lines, the number of poor people has fallen much less, or has even risen.
  • Relative or absolute? Has the share of poor people fallen, or their number? Measured against what is needed to live a decent life where people live?

What do newer data say?

Gradín (2024) revisits global inequality trends from 1980 to 2020. He confirms that global inequality declined in relative terms since about 2000, mainly because of strong growth in China, India, and other emerging economies. In absolute terms, however, inequality kept rising.

ImportantKey takeaways of chapter 2 so far
  • In terms of dollar distances between people (absolute inequality), inequality has unequivocally increased, both between and within countries.
  • Global inequality in relative terms has declined since about 2000, mainly due to strong growth in China, India, and other emerging economies.
  • The exceptions are at the extremes of the distribution: stagnation among the very poor and rising concentration among the very rich.

2.4 Beyond Income: A Case Study and Your Own Graph

Case study: growth and poverty in Laos

Lao PDR is a good example of why the choice of measure matters. Between 2003 and 2013, the country recorded high economic growth and impressive poverty reduction: measured with the international monetary poverty line of USD 1.90 a day, poverty fell by about 40%.

But who benefited? A multidimensional analysis of poverty, which looks at deprivations in education, health, and living standards rather than income alone, tells a more mixed story (Bader et al. 2016, 2017):

  • Monetary and multidimensional poverty often do not identify the same people as poor. Policies targeting only the monetary poor miss many deprived households.
  • Over the same period, malnutrition remained widespread: according to the WHO, around 40% of children under five were stunted, a level of undernutrition that impairs development.
  • Inequalities within Laos are as large as global inequalities, and they follow horizontal lines: ethnic minorities in remote upland areas benefited far less from growth than the lowland majority.

Growth reduced income poverty, but it also increased disparities. A single monetary indicator hid both the persistence of non-income deprivation and the growing gaps between groups.

Exercise: My inequality graph

NoteExercise (preparation for the live session)
  1. Go to the World Inequality Database and explore its World view and Country graphs.
  2. Pick one graph or picture that tells an interesting story about inequality: one you find illuminating, surprising, or confusing.
  3. Write two or three sentences explaining your choice: What does the graph show? Which story does it tell?
  4. Then analyse it with the concepts of this module: Does it show relative or absolute inequality? Inequality within or between countries? Does it suggest an individual or structural explanation? What does it leave out?

Winter school participants: post your graph and sentences on the session’s Miro board before the session (link on the Live session page).

TipWhat a good answer covers
  • States precisely what is measured: income or wealth, before or after taxes, which population and which years.
  • Identifies whether the graph shows relative shares, ratios, or absolute amounts, and how the story might change with the other perspective.
  • Names the reference unit (within a country, between countries, global).
  • Notes at least one thing the graph cannot show, such as non-income dimensions, horizontal inequalities, or what happens within the top 1%.
  • Links the graph to one of the two stories of this chapter.
