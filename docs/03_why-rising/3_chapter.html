3  Why Is Inequality Rising?

We have seen what inequality looks like. Now we ask why it has been rising in most countries since the 1980s. There is no single explanation: economists, sociologists, and political scientists offer different narratives. This chapter starts with the most common mainstream explanation and then moves to structural and political economy accounts. The central question is whether rising inequality is a natural market outcome or whether it is produced.

3.1 The Conventional Wisdom

The standard explanation sounds neutral, almost technical. It rests on two forces.

Skill-biased technological change. Technological change transforms markets and the way we work. Automation and digitalisation increase the demand for highly skilled workers and reduce the demand for routine labour. When education does not keep pace with technology, skilled workers become scarce, and the wage gap between them and less skilled workers grows. Today, digitalisation and artificial intelligence are starting to replace human labour at higher skill levels too.

Globalisation. Capital became mobile: firms can relocate production, outsource services, or automate. Labour remained largely national. Workers in rich countries were exposed to competition with workers in lower-wage countries, which put pressure on wages and labour standards.

NoteExample: the car industry in Detroit

Car manufacturing used to rely on manual labour. Many assembly-line jobs did not require advanced education and paid enough to support a family. As robots took over painting, welding, and assembly, far fewer workers were needed on the factory floor, while engineers, technicians, and programmers were in high demand. Combined with competition from lower-wage locations, this hollowed out whole regions of the US, which became known as the “Rust Belt.”

In this story, inequality is the result of education gaps and global competition, not of power. The conclusion is that rising inequality is essentially a natural market process: you may not like it, but there is little you can or should do about it, except invest in skills and let the market adjust. The underlying assumption is that free markets pay workers according to their contribution.

Robert Reich, former US Secretary of Labor, argues that this conventional wisdom is not wrong, but that it leaves out some of the largest and most important changes. The next sections look at them.

3.2 Choosing an Analytical Lens

Chapter 1 showed that our concepts shape what we see. Before looking at further explanations, it helps to be explicit about the lens this module uses.

Element Choice in this module
Concept of justice The capability approach (Sen, Nussbaum), complemented by collectivist perspectives that ask about the structures of society (for example Sandel)
Approach Systemic and structural: inequality is analysed as an outcome of institutions, rules, and power relations, not only of individual attributes (Bader and Bieri 2023)

Even if technology matters, it operates within a social structure. People, firms, and governments decide who owns new technologies and who benefits from them. Inequality is therefore shaped by institutions and power, not only by markets.

A different lens would lead to different findings. A purely individual approach would focus on education and effort; a libertarian lens would see little need for explanation at all as long as exchanges are voluntary. Being transparent about the lens is part of good analysis.

3.3 The Weakening Redistributive State

Many factors affect the distribution of income and wealth, and a large part of them are political. A first structural explanation concerns the state.

In OECD economies, taxes and transfers typically reduced the inequality of disposable income by one fifth to one quarter compared with market income (Dabla-Norris et al. 2015). In recent decades, this redistributive role has shrunk, for three reasons:

  • Reduced progressivity of personal income taxes: top marginal tax rates were cut sharply in most rich countries from the late 1970s onwards.
  • Lower taxes on capital and corporations, often justified as necessary for international competitiveness.
  • Tighter spending on social programmes due to austerity policies.

The effect is a redistribution upwards, concentrating income and wealth among those who own capital.

Rich countries, poor governments

The World Inequality Report 2022 shows a striking pattern: over the past 40 years, countries have become much richer, but their governments have become much poorer. In rich countries, the share of wealth held by public actors is close to zero or even negative, so that almost all wealth is in private hands. The COVID-19 crisis amplified this trend, as governments borrowed the equivalent of 10 to 20% of GDP, essentially from the private sector (Chancel et al. 2022). Poorer governments have less capacity to provide education, health, and security, and private companies increasingly take their place.

Commodification

There are two basic ways in which people get access to goods and services:

  • They are provided as a right of membership in a group, for example public health care or education for all citizens or residents.
  • They are purchased or otherwise secured by those with money.

Commodification is the process by which goods, services, and opportunities are increasingly allocated to those who can pay. The more essential things, such as housing, health, care, or education, are commodified, the more income inequality translates into inequality of capabilities. Rising commodification drives inequality and narrows the scope for state action.

ImportantThe core point

Inequality outcomes can be, and are, mediated by policies, rules, and legislation. Inequality is thus produced. To understand it, we need to analyse what has changed in these mediators.

3.4 Countervailing Powers and the Use of Profits

Countervailing powers

In 1952, the economist John Kenneth Galbraith introduced the idea of countervailing powers: trade unions, cooperatives, small retailers, local banks, and public regulation that balance the power of large corporations. Capitalism, he argued, works in a socially acceptable way only when such counterweights exist. Since the 1980s, many of them have been weakened or dismantled: union membership has declined, labour markets were deregulated, and market concentration has grown.

Robert Reich (2020) describes the result as a vicious cycle: big money from large corporations, financial markets, and the wealthy shapes the rules of the economic and social game, and these rules in turn favour the top at the expense of everyone else. From this perspective, rising inequality is not only a market failure; it is also a policy success for those who benefit from it.

Productivity and wages

Mainstream theory says that wages reflect productivity: if workers produce more, they should earn more. For the decades after the Second World War, often called the “golden age of capitalism,” real wages in rich countries indeed tracked productivity.

Since then, a gap has opened. The International Labour Organization reports that in 52 high-income countries, labour productivity grew by 21.8% between 1999 and 2019, while real wages grew by only 14.3% (ILO 2020). The benefits of higher productivity are increasingly captured by the owners of capital rather than passed on to workers. Globally, the share of national income going to labour has declined (ILO 2019).

NoteHypothesis

The abolition of countervailing powers such as trade unions and the weakening redistributive role of the state have contributed to the gap between productivity growth and real wage growth in high-income countries.

Where do the profits go?

If productivity gains do not go to wages, where do they go? William Lazonick distinguishes two corporate strategies:

Retain and invest (value creation) Downsize and distribute (value extraction)
Dominant from the 1940s to the 1970s Dominant since the 1980s
Profits are reinvested: expanding skills, raising pay, hiring people, creating new products Profits are paid out to shareholders through dividends and share buybacks; jobs become insecure and executive pay explodes
Real wages track productivity Erosion of middle-class employment

In a share buyback, a company purchases its own shares on the market. This reduces the number of shares, increases earnings per share, and typically lifts the share price, which benefits shareholders and executives paid in stock (Lazonick 2014). Some large companies have spent more than 100% of their profits on buybacks and dividends over several years, borrowing to pay out their shareholders. An analysis of Apple’s annual reports for 2012 to 2018 by the author of this module found such a pattern.

This links back to Kalecki (section 1.4): when profits rise but are not reinvested, demand weakens and growth slows. Financialisation, the growing weight of financial markets and motives in the economy, rewards existing wealth rather than productive activity and thereby amplifies inequality.

3.5 How Wealth Concentrates: Synthesis

So far, we have mostly looked at income, a flow. Wealth is a stock: accumulated, often inherited, harder to see, and politically less contested. Wealth inequality is systematically larger than income inequality in every region of the world (Chancel et al. 2022). Why does wealth concentrate so persistently?

r > g

Thomas Piketty (2014) summarised one mechanism in a short formula: r > g. When the rate of return on capital (r) is higher than the growth rate of the economy (g), wealth grows faster than incomes from work, and those who already own capital pull ahead. Historically, r has exceeded g most of the time. The main exceptions were the decades after the two World Wars, when destruction, inflation, high taxes, and strong unions compressed inequality.

For Piketty, this is not a law of nature. Inequality, he argues in Capital and Ideology (2020), is ideological and political: markets, profits, wages, capital, debt, and tax havens are social constructs that depend on the legal, tax, and political systems a society chooses.

Matthew effects

Socio-economic research identifies cumulative advantage, or Matthew effects (“for whoever has, to him more will be given”): those with high incomes can save a larger share of their income than those with low incomes, and capital owners often earn higher returns than small savers. Both effects reinforce each other. Even pure chance can produce extreme concentration: if wealth grows by a random percentage each year, a small group ends up owning a large share over time, even without any systematic advantage. In such processes, the “average” outcome says little about what a typical person experiences.

Synthesis

Based on the analytical lens of this module, rising inequality has several interacting causes:

  • Technological change has increased productivity, but not necessarily fair rewards.
  • Globalisation has shifted power from labour to capital.
  • Financialisation and asset inflation have enriched those who were already wealthy.
  • Institutional weakening has removed the counterweights that once protected workers and citizens.

Together, these forces have created a system in which inequality is self-reinforcing. And because it is produced by rules and institutions, it can also be changed.
