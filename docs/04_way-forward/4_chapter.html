4  The Way Forward: Politics of Inequality

We have analysed what inequality is, how it has developed, and why it is rising. Now comes the critical question: what can be done? If inequality is produced by rules and institutions, it can be reduced by changing them. Addressing inequality requires more than redistribution after the fact. It means rethinking how value is created, who owns productive assets, and how power is shared. This chapter presents five directions, building on the analytical lens of chapter 3, and ends by asking where you stand.

4.1 Hardly the Silver Bullets: Growth and Education

For decades, policymakers assumed that two things would fix inequality: economic growth and better education. Growth was seen as a tide that lifts all boats; education as the great equaliser.

Growth. As chapter 2 showed, growth can reduce income poverty. But growth is meaningless for reducing inequality if its gains keep going to the top 1% and little trickles down. In a world facing planetary boundaries, betting on ever more growth to solve distributional questions is also ecologically risky.

Education. Access to schools and better education has been, and will remain, an important lever towards more equal societies. But it does not do the trick alone. In The Tyranny of Merit (2020), Michael Sandel challenges the education argument. Based on the US, he describes a society built on the belief that “what you earn will depend on what you learn”:

  • Those at the top come to believe that their success is their own doing, a pure measure of their merit.
  • Those at the bottom have no one to blame but themselves.

This generates hubris among the winners and humiliation among the losers, and prepares the ground for authoritarian populism. Sandel also notes that stagnating wages despite rising productivity were not caused by workers’ lack of know-how, but by their lack of power (see section 3.4). Elizabeth Anderson makes a related point: productivity attaches mainly to work roles and to the capital put at a worker’s disposal, not to individuals. More education alone leads to credential inflation rather than to more equality.

4.2 Improve the Diagnosis

Policies can only be as good as the diagnosis they rest on. A first step is to substantiate the story of inequality with measures that are multidimensional, sensitive to the extremes, and inclusive of groups:

  • Poverty: multidimensional poverty measures look at deprivations in education, health, and living standards, not only income. As the Laos case showed (section 2.4), they often identify different people as poor than monetary measures do (Bader et al. 2016).
  • Inequality: the Palma ratio (section 1.3) and the Inequality-adjusted Human Development Index (IHDI) of UNDP, which discounts a country’s human development achievements by the inequality in their distribution.
  • Horizontal inequalities: data broken down by ethnicity, region, gender, or age make structural exclusion visible.
  • Wealth and the top: better data on wealth, inheritances, and top incomes, which household surveys tend to miss.

Such measures should be complemented by bottom-up, grounded experiences of what it means to be poor or excluded, and by tools to assess the distributional impact of a broad set of policies, including economic and fiscal policies. They build powerful arguments about inequality and how best to fight it.

4.3 Beyond the Invisible Hand: Who Creates Value?

Behind many debates on inequality lies a deeper belief: that the market is the best allocation mechanism, that market outcomes are objectively fair, and that the profits companies make are therefore entirely their own doing. From this belief follows that the state should not tax them much.

Mariana Mazzucato challenges this belief. In The Entrepreneurial State (2013) and Mission Economy (2021), she shows that many of the innovations underpinning modern economies, from the internet and GPS to the touchscreen and mRNA vaccines, would not exist without state-funded, high-risk research. The state does not only fix market failures; it actively creates and shapes markets. Yet the risks of innovation are often public, while the returns are private. The return on public investment, in this view, comes through taxes, public ownership stakes, or conditions attached to public funding.

This leads to three proposals:

  • Break up the myth that only business creates value. The economy is co-produced by public investment, private entrepreneurship, care work, and collective infrastructure. (Until the 1960s, banking and finance were not even counted as productive in national accounts; what counts as “value” is itself a political decision.)
  • Correct the fiscal bias in favour of capital. Today, labour is heavily taxed, and social security systems depend on it, which makes labour expensive. This creates incentives towards “excessive automation” that destroys jobs without necessarily increasing productivity (Brynjolfsson et al. 2020).
  • Redirect technological progress towards resource productivity rather than labour productivity: saving energy and materials rather than people.

4.4 From Redistribution to Fair Pre-distribution

Fiscal policy is not merely technical. It is a moral and political choice about what kind of society we want. Two complementary approaches exist.

Redistribution corrects market outcomes after the fact, through taxes and transfers:

  • higher taxes on capital, data, and resources relative to labour (Acosta 2017)
  • inheritance taxes, to counter the concentration of wealth and political power across generations (plutocracy)
  • international cooperation against tax avoidance: in 2021, more than 130 countries agreed on a global minimum tax of 15% for large multinational companies (OECD)

Pre-distribution aims to make the distribution of market incomes itself more equal, before taxes (Hacker 2011; Bozio et al. 2020). It acts on bargaining power, wage-setting rules, corporate law, and trade regimes:

  • limits on top pay, for example relative to the median wage in a company
  • living wages and job guarantees for the lowest-paid
  • broader ownership of capital, for example through employee stock ownership plans
  • democratising companies
  • universal basic services

Pre-distribution has gained support since the crises of recent years. After the 2007/08 financial crisis, governments argued they had no choice but to cut services. The COVID-19 pandemic showed that states do have other options: austerity was halted almost overnight and unprecedented fiscal resources were mobilised.

Re-embedding the economy

Karl Polanyi warned that when markets become “disembedded” from society, social and ecological crises follow. Two proposals aim to re-embed the economy.

Spreading ownership of capital. An employee stock ownership plan enables employees to own part or all of the company they work for. Yanis Varoufakis (2020) sketches a more radical version: every employee receives one share when hired, which cannot be sold, and which gives one vote. All major decisions are taken collectively, with the intranet acting as a permanent shareholders’ assembly. Equal ownership does not mean equal pay: employees decide together how revenues are split between costs, investment, basic pay, and bonuses. As Varoufakis asks, why should executives be paid in company shares, but not workers?

Universal basic services (UBS). Unlike a universal basic income, which pays cash, UBS focuses on collective provision of what everyone needs: housing, health care, education, care, mobility, and digital access (Coote and Percy 2020; Gough 2020). Its principles:

  • Services: collectively generated activities that serve the public interest
  • Basic: essential and sufficient to meet needs, not a minimal safety net for the “left behind”
  • Universal: everyone is entitled, regardless of their ability to pay

UBS counteracts commodification (section 3.3). It also links social and ecological goals: human needs, unlike wants, are limited and satiable, so meeting everyone’s needs within planetary boundaries is possible. Many services depend on people and relationships rather than on energy-intensive hardware. Examples range from public housing in Vienna to free public transport in Luxembourg and childcare in the Nordic countries.

4.5 Be Clear About Your Position

Where do incomes come from?

Your position in relation to the means of production (section 1.4) still matters. In rich countries, the median household receives most of its income, around 70 to 80%, from wages and salaries, and most of the rest from pensions and social security. For the top 0.01%, the picture is reversed: around 80% of their income comes from capital gains, interest, and dividends, and only about 15% from wages and salaries (SSRC n.d.).

Milanovic (2025) estimates that 77% of households in advanced and middle-income economies have zero or close to zero cash income from capital. If so few people own financial and productive capital, the gains from new technologies such as artificial intelligence, which raise the importance of capital, will accrue to those who already own it. Inequality would rise even if nobody became poorer.

Information revolution

Most people underestimate how unequal their societies are, and they tend to rank their own position higher than it actually is. Mijs (2021) finds a paradox: in more unequal societies, citizens are more likely to explain inequality in meritocratic terms, while people in more equal countries are more likely to see structural causes. Inequality and the belief in meritocracy go hand in hand.

Rising inequality does not seem to be a problem people constantly worry about. Awareness raising and better information could make it more tangible. If the structures and reward systems that reproduce inequality become visible, political preferences may shift and open the way for new initiatives in pre- and redistribution.

ImportantKey takeaways of chapter 4
  • Growth and education are important, but hardly silver bullets.
  • Better diagnosis comes first: multidimensional, group-sensitive measures and better data on wealth.
  • Value is co-created by the public and private sectors; tax systems should reflect this.
  • Pre-distribution (ownership, bargaining power, pay rules, universal basic services) complements redistribution.
  • Where you stand shapes what you see: be clear about your own position.
